Personal finance model

Finance.

A year-by-year projection of your own numbers, and a few thousand randomized versions of it, so the plan is described by a range instead of a single confident line.

Stored only in this browser. Nothing is uploaded. Never enter account numbers.

Levers

Drag to see what actually moves the outcome. Changes are saved to your plan, so put a lever back where you found it if you were only looking.

Where the money sits

Balances by bucket over time, in today's dollars and on an after-tax basis — pre-tax accounts are shown discounted by the withdrawal rate, taxable accounts net of embedded gains tax. This is the deterministic path: one set of point-estimate assumptions, no randomness.

The range of outcomes

Every randomized path, summarized as percentile bands, with the deterministic path drawn over the top. If the deterministic line sits well above the median band, the point-estimate projection is flattering you.

What this model does not know

Before you trust any number on this screen

Every figure below ships as an illustrative placeholder. They are round numbers chosen to make the model run, not advice and not anyone's real situation. Replace each one with your own figure, then mark it verified with a note on where it came from — six months from now that note is the difference between a number you can rely on and a number you have to re-derive.

    Income streams

    One row per stream: salary, a partner's salary, self-employment, rental income, a pension. Amounts are in today's dollars and grow at their own rate from now, so a stream that starts later still arrives having grown. Mark clearly whether a figure is gross or take-home — conflating the two is the easiest error to make here.

    Goals

    Anything that costs money on a schedule: a home purchase, tuition, a sabbatical, supporting a family member, a car. Give a goal an end year to repeat it every year in the range, which is how a temporary expense should be modeled.

    Year-by-year ledger

    The deterministic projection in full. This is the artifact worth reading closely: if a year looks wrong here, the charts everywhere else are wrong in the same way.

    Percentile fan

    After-tax net worth in today's dollars across every simulated path.

    Property is not simulated: its value follows the deterministic path in every scenario, so including it narrows the visible spread without adding any real certainty. Turn it off to see the fan for money you could actually spend.

    Share of paths still solvent

    The fraction of simulated paths that still hold spendable assets at each age. Read the age where this line starts bending, not just the number at the end.

    Where paths end up

    Final after-tax net worth in today's dollars, one bar per band of outcomes. The long right tail is why the average outcome is a bad planning number and the median is a better one.

    Simulation settings

    How the paths are generated, and which risks are switched on. The optional ones are off by default because each is a real judgment call about how much realism is worth the added complexity.

    Saved scenarios

    Freeze the current plan under a name, change something, and compare. Retiring two years earlier and spending 10% less are the two levers that usually dominate everything else, and this is where you find out by how much.

    Why the model looks the way it does

    Current state only, generated from your inputs and the notes you wrote when you verified them. This is deliberately not a changelog: the history of how a number evolved lives in the project's git history, where the diffs are real and never drift out of sync with the code.

    Structural choices worth knowing about

    Known limitations

    Accepted simplifications, disclosed rather than hidden. A model that is honest about what it leaves out is far more useful than one that quietly implies precision it does not have. These are different from the Open items tab: a limitation is something currently accepted, an open item is something that should actually get resolved.

    IRS Uniform Lifetime Table

    Public IRS data, used to size required minimum distributions: the start-of-year pre-tax balance divided by the distribution period for that age.

    Research this yourself

    These are the parts of the model that cannot be inherited from a template. Tax law changes, thresholds move, and treatment varies by state and city — so each item below needs checking against current rules for your own situation, and the placeholder rates in the model are worth exactly nothing until you do.

    Open items

    Things that need a decision, a real number, or a methodology improvement. When one gets resolved, delete it and put the answer in the source note on the field it was about, so the two never describe the same open question.

    Notes

    Scratch space that travels with the plan when you export it.

    A projection is not a prediction. Every number here follows from assumptions you supplied, and the ones about tax, returns, and how long you live are the ones most likely to be wrong.

    Not financial, tax, or legal advice. Data lives in this browser's local storage on this device — export it if you would mind losing it.
